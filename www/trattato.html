Trattato del Forno

How a website might behave if it were a pizzeria in Naples in the Italian Renaissance.

The honest anachronism

Pizza, as a tomato pie with a name, is a nineteenth-century Neapolitan invention. The Renaissance oven was a bread oven. What Naples already had was fire, dough, oil, the street, and the sea. A site in this spirit does not pretend the Margherita was served to a viceroy in 1520. It asks a different question: if a forno on a vicolo had to greet a stranger the way a letter, a shop sign, and a table would, what would the pages be?

Florence gives us courts, medals, and gold leaf. Naples gives us tuff stone, a crowded street, Spanish rule, the gulf, and a kitchen that works in public. The site should smell like the second city, not a Medici chapel with a pepperoni overlay.

The house, not the funnel

Modern pizzeria sites are built to take an order. A Renaissance house is built to receive a guest. The first screen is a doorway: who keeps the fire, whether it is lit, and whether you are welcome. The invitation we already wrote is that doorway. What follows are rooms, not checkout steps.

  • Porta Home. The oven and the street. Our kitchen video is the fire seen from the pass — not a stock slice spinning in space.
  • Invito The letter. A guest is named. Mr. Bojangles keeps the table. RSVP is a reply to a host, not a cart.
  • Tavola The bill of fare. Today that is the raw bar we were given: half oyster, named waters. Tomorrow, if we invent pies, they get proper names, like a mustard pie or a bar pie — never “our famous pizza.”
  • Bottega The gallery. Hands, plates, the room at dusk. Pictures hang like panels in a workshop, not a grid of specials.
  • Lettera Contact. The same reservation note we already have: write when you would come; a reply within half an hour.

What to steal from living shops

From the New Jersey rooms we read: put the host, the hours, and the way to speak to the house on the first screen. Give the signature dish a name. Tell the truth about cash, closed Mondays, or a fire. Heritage is a date and a family, not a texture overlay.

What not to steal: a floating Order button, a third-party bag, a PDF that dies on a phone. Those are useful in Rahway. They are wrong in a vicolo.

The table of the gulf

Bread, oil, and the sea first. Tomato, if it appears, arrives as a New World guest — late, loud, and honest about the century it belongs to.

That is why the oyster list is not a mistake on a “pizza” site. Naples eats the gulf. A Renaissance forno next to the water would put the catch beside the loaf. The photographs we already have — the wooden table, the steam, the kitchen pass — are the bottega. We do not need to replace them with a cartoon pie.

How it should feel to move

Type like a humanist book: Roman capitals for the name of the house, italic for speech. Color like stone, wine, gold leaf, and the dark of a vaulted room. Motion slow enough to be a curtain, not a carousel of deals. The guest scrolls as if turning a quarto. Buttons are seals and replies. Nothing blinks.

On a small screen the street still comes first: name, fire, invitation, then the rooms stacked as a single vicolo. No hamburger that hides the host.

What we have not built yet

This page is the thinking, not the finished house. Still to draw: a Porta that is more doorway than sidebar, hours spoken as when the oven is lit, a map that is a street fragment rather than a pin, and a tavola that can hold bread and sea without pretending the year is 1889.

The invitation already knows the tone. The rest of the site should walk in the same shoes.
