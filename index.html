Separation of Powers

The Three Branches of Government

The United States Constitution divides federal power among three branches — one to make the laws, one to enforce them, and one to interpret them — so that no single branch can dominate the others.

“The accumulation of all powers, legislative, executive, and judiciary, in the same hands… may justly be pronounced the very definition of tyranny.” — James Madison, Federalist No. 47

Article I

Legislative Branch

Makes the laws

Congress is a bicameral legislature: the House of Representatives (435 voting members, apportioned by population, two-year terms) and the Senate (100 members, two per state, six-year terms).

Key powers

  • Writes and passes federal laws
  • Controls taxing and spending (“power of the purse”)
  • Declares war and regulates commerce
  • Confirms appointments and ratifies treaties (Senate)
535 voting members of Congress

Article II

Executive Branch

Enforces the laws

Headed by the President, elected to a four-year term (limited to two terms by the 22nd Amendment), along with the Vice President, the Cabinet, and federal agencies that carry out the law.

Key powers

  • Signs or vetoes legislation
  • Commander in Chief of the armed forces
  • Negotiates treaties and conducts foreign policy
  • Appoints judges, ambassadors, and officials
1 President, limited to two elected terms

Article III

Judicial Branch

Interprets the laws

The Supreme Court and lower federal courts decide what laws mean and whether they follow the Constitution. Justices serve for life during “good behaviour.”

Key powers

  • Interprets laws and the Constitution
  • Judicial review — can strike down unconstitutional laws (Marbury v. Madison, 1803)
  • Resolves disputes between states
  • Hears cases involving federal law and treaties
