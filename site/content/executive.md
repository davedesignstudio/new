## One office, many hands

Article II is shorter than Article I, and more unfinished. It names a President and a Vice President, describes election (later rewritten by the Twelfth Amendment), and then speaks in verbs: take care, receive ambassadors, commission officers, grant pardons, make treaties, nominate judges.

The Cabinet is a custom, not a clause. Departments exist because Congress created them. The President directs them. That is the whole theory of a unitary executive, argued over in every generation: how far do those verbs reach?

## Powers that belong to the President

- **Commander in chief** of the Army, Navy, and the militia when called into service. Congress declares war and funds the forces; the President directs campaigns.
- **The take-care clause.** Laws do not enforce themselves. The President must “take Care that the Laws be faithfully executed.”
- **Appointment.** Ambassadors, judges, and principal officers — with the Senate’s advice and consent. Inferior officers may be appointed as Congress directs.
- **The veto.** A signed objection returns a bill to Congress. Two-thirds of each house may still enact it.
- **Pardons** for federal offenses, except in impeachment.
- **Foreign recognition.** Receiving ambassadors is how the United States decides which governments exist.

Executive orders are not a separate constitutional power. They are written instructions to the executive branch, valid only when a statute or the Constitution already supplies authority.

## Limits that belong to everyone else

The President cannot write a statute, levy a tax, create a department, or spend money Congress refused. Treaties need two-thirds of the Senate. Appointments need a majority. War powers are shared and, in practice, contested. Impeachment hangs over the office as a political remedy for “Treason, Bribery, or other high Crimes and Misdemeanors.”

The Twenty-second Amendment, ratified in 1951, limits a President to two elected terms. The Twenty-fifth provides for disability and succession after the Vice President.

## The Vice President

The Vice President is first a spare President and second the President of the Senate. For most of American history the office was ceremonial. Modern Vice Presidents sit in the Situation Room, break 50–50 Senate ties, and wait — by design — one heartbeat away.

## What “the executive branch” really is

Beyond the White House: fifteen Cabinet departments, independent agencies Congress insulated from direct removal, the uniformed services, U.S. attorneys, and a civil service hired under laws the President does not get to rewrite at will. Energy in the executive is real. So is the thicket of statutes that tell that energy where it may run.
