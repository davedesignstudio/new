## One office, a vast machine

Article II is shorter than Article I, and more spare. It vests “the executive Power” in a single President — not a council, not a committee. The Framers wanted a person who could act with “energy,” especially in foreign affairs and emergencies, and who could be held to account at the next election.

That one officer now sits atop a sprawling establishment: the Vice President, the Cabinet, the Executive Office of the President, and the independent and executive agencies that write rules, spend money, and apply federal law every day.

The President is elected to a four-year term, with a two-term limit under the Twenty-Second Amendment. The Vice President is first in the line of succession and presides over the Senate, breaking ties.

## What the President may do

The Constitution names several core powers:

- **Commander in chief** of the Army and Navy, and of the militia when called into federal service
- Take care that the laws be faithfully executed
- Nominate ambassadors, public ministers, judges, and other officers — with the Senate’s advice and consent
- Make treaties, provided two-thirds of the Senate concur
- Grant reprieves and pardons for federal offenses, except in cases of impeachment
- Receive ambassadors (the power to recognize foreign governments)
- Convene one or both houses of Congress on extraordinary occasions
- Veto legislation

Presidents also issue **executive orders** and presidential memoranda. Those instruments can direct the executive branch, but they cannot create new law out of nothing. They stand only as far as a statute or the Constitution allows, and courts can set them aside.

## Faithful execution — and its limits

The Take Care Clause is both a grant and a duty. The President must enforce the laws Congress has written, not only the ones the White House prefers. Selective non-enforcement has always been part of governing a large country; wholesale refusal to execute a valid statute is another matter, and it is a recurring fight among the branches.

War powers sit on the same fault line. Congress declares war and appropriates; the President commands. Since 1945, most uses of force have not been preceded by a formal declaration. The War Powers Resolution of 1973 tries to pull the President back toward Congress; presidents of both parties have treated it as advisory as often as binding.

## How the executive checks the others

The veto is the most visible check on Congress. A regular veto returns the bill; a pocket veto can kill a measure at the end of a session. Appointments let a President shape the courts for decades. Pardons can undo federal prosecutions — a check on the judiciary and on the Department of Justice, and one of the least reviewable powers in the Constitution.

The executive cannot dissolve Congress, write the budget by decree, or ignore a final court order. Energy was the point. Unlimited energy was the fear.

## The building

The public face of the office is the **White House**. The work of the branch is also done in the Eisenhower Executive Office Building, the Cabinet departments, and agencies from the Pentagon to the EPA. Article II names a person. The modern executive is a government inside the government.
