## Why two chambers?

The Framers did not trust a single assembly. The House, with two-year terms and seats apportioned by population, is built to feel elections quickly. The Senate, with six-year terms and two seats for every state, is built to slow the House down. A bill must survive both tempers before it is a law.

That bargain — sometimes called the Connecticut Compromise — is why Wyoming and California meet as equals in one chamber and as unequals in the other.

## The House of Representatives

The House has 435 voting members, a number fixed by statute since 1929, plus non-voting delegates from territories and the District of Columbia. Seats are reapportioned after every census.

The House holds three powers that begin here and nowhere else:

- **Revenue bills.** Taxes and borrowing start in the chamber closest to the voters.
- **Impeachment.** The House alone may accuse a President, judge, or civil officer.
- **Contested elections.** It is the judge of its own members’ elections and qualifications.

The Speaker — chosen by the House — sets the chamber’s agenda more firmly than any single senator can set the Senate’s.

## The Senate

One hundred senators serve staggered six-year terms, so only about one-third face the voters in any national election. The Vice President presides and breaks ties. The Senate’s distinctive checks are:

- **Advice and consent.** Treaties (by two-thirds) and most principal officers, including judges.
- **Impeachment trials.** After the House accuses, the Senate sits as a court. Conviction takes two-thirds.
- **Filibuster practice.** The Constitution requires simple majorities for ordinary legislation; the Senate’s own rules have often demanded more. Those rules are not in the Constitution and can be changed by the Senate itself.

## What Congress may do

Article I, Section 8 lists the enumerated powers: tax and spend, borrow, regulate interstate and foreign commerce, coin money, establish post offices and lower courts, raise armies and a navy, declare war, and make all laws “necessary and proper” for carrying those powers into execution.

Section 9 lists what Congress may *not* do — no bills of attainder, no ex post facto laws, no titles of nobility. The Bill of Rights later added a thicker wall around speech, religion, arms, searches, and criminal procedure.

## How a member actually works

Most of the work is committee work: hearings, markups, oversight letters, and the quiet bargaining that never reaches C-SPAN. Leadership offices decide what reaches the floor. The Congressional Budget Office scores costs. The Government Accountability Office audits the executive. None of those offices is named in Article I; they exist because Congress invented tools to keep up with a modern state.

## The check that never sleeps

Lawmaking is intermittent. Oversight is constant. Subpoenas, confirmation hearings, appropriations riders, and inspectors general are how Congress stays in the other branches’ business between votes on famous bills. The sharpest tool remains the oldest: Congress must appropriate the money before the executive may spend it.
