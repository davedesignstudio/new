## Two chambers, one Congress

The legislative branch is **bicameral**: a law must usually clear both the House of Representatives and the Senate before it can reach the President’s desk.

The **House** is built to feel the public quickly. It has 435 voting members, apportioned by population, each serving a two-year term. Revenue bills start here. The House also has the sole power to impeach federal officers.

The **Senate** is built to slow the temperature. It has 100 members — two from every state, regardless of size — serving staggered six-year terms. The Senate tries impeachments, confirms presidential appointments, and approves treaties by a two-thirds vote.

Together they are Congress. Neither chamber is the legislature by itself.

## What Article I actually grants

Section 8 is the working list. Among other things, Congress may:

- Lay and collect taxes, borrow money, and spend for the general welfare
- Regulate commerce among the states and with foreign nations
- Coin money and establish post offices
- Raise and support armies, provide and maintain a navy, and **declare war**
- Constitute tribunals inferior to the Supreme Court
- Make all laws “necessary and proper” for carrying out its powers

These are **enumerated** powers. The Tenth Amendment reserves what is not delegated to the states or the people. In practice, the Commerce Clause, the spending power, and the Necessary and Proper Clause have been the widest doors.

## How a bill becomes a law

1. A member introduces a bill. Most die in committee.
2. If a committee reports it, the chamber debates, amends, and votes.
3. The other chamber must pass the same text — often after a conference.
4. The President may sign it, let it become law without a signature, or veto it.
5. Congress can override a veto with a **two-thirds vote in both houses**.

The President cannot write statutes. Agencies can issue regulations, but those rest on authority Congress has already given.

## How Congress checks the others

Congress does not merely legislate. It holds the **power of the purse**: no money may be drawn from the Treasury except by appropriation. That is a hard limit on presidents and on wars.

It also investigates, subpoenas, and — in rare cases — impeaches. The Senate’s advice-and-consent power shapes the Cabinet and the entire federal bench. And if the Supreme Court reads a statute in a way Congress rejects, Congress can rewrite the statute (it cannot rewrite the Constitution by ordinary majority).

## The building

The work happens in the **United States Capitol**. The House sits in the south wing; the Senate in the north. The dome in between is a reminder that the two chambers are meant to be one Congress — argumentative, slow, and public.
