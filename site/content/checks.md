## Not three silos

Separation of powers would be a tidy org chart: Congress writes, the President executes, the courts interpret. The Constitution is less tidy on purpose. Each branch is given tools that reach into the others’ work. Those tools are the **checks**. The resulting tension is the **balance**.

Madison did not trust parchment barriers. He trusted rival interests. A President who wants a law will still need votes. A Congress that wants a war will still need a commander. A Court that wants a doctrine will still need a case, and then compliance.

## Congress checking the President

The purse is the oldest check. Armies expire without appropriation. Agencies stop if the money stops. Oversight hearings, inspectors general, and the Government Accountability Office are later machinery built on that same idea: the executive spends what the legislature allows.

Impeachment is the emergency brake. The House accuses; the Senate tries; conviction requires two-thirds. It has been used rarely against presidents and more often against judges. Removal is a political judgment as well as a legal one — which is why the Framers put it in Congress, not the courts.

Treaties and nominations give the Senate a standing role in foreign policy and in the composition of the government. A President who cannot get a Cabinet confirmed is a President who cannot fully staff the branch.

## The President checking Congress

The veto forces a supermajority. Most bills that survive both chambers still need a signature. The regular veto can be overridden; the threat of it shapes drafting long before a vote.

The President also sets the agenda in quieter ways: the State of the Union, the budget proposal, the choice of what to enforce first. None of those replace legislation. All of them change what Congress finds itself answering.

## The courts checking both

Judicial review is the check that does not look like force. A statute can pass both houses, survive a veto, and still fall because it contradicts a higher law. An executive order can be stayed nationwide by a single district judge, then fought up the ladder.

This is why the appointment power matters so much. Courts check the political branches; the political branches choose the judges. The loop is closed on purpose.

## The political branches checking the courts

Congress can change the size and structure of the lower courts. It can strip certain kinds of appellate jurisdiction — a power that exists on paper and is used gingerly, because it looks like punishing a result. It can propose constitutional amendments that overrule a decision (the Eleventh, Fourteenth, Sixteenth, and Twenty-Sixth Amendments all answer the Court in different ways).

The President nominates every Article III judge and can, in theory, decline to enforce a ruling. That last option is a constitutional crisis, not a routine check. The ordinary path is to appoint different judges and wait.

## What the system is not

It is not efficiency. Bills stall. Agencies are sued. Confirmations take years. The Framers accepted friction as the price of avoiding a single point of failure.

It is also not a guarantee of virtue. Checks work only when the people who hold them are willing to use them — and when voters notice when they are not. Federalist 51 assumed ambition. It did not assume that the ambitious would always aim at the public good.

The three branches are a design. Keeping them is a practice.
