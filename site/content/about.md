## What this is

A plain-language reference to the structure of the United States federal government: what each of the three branches does, how its members get there, and how the branches limit one another. It is written for students, new citizens, and anyone who wants the mechanics without the commentary.

Everything here describes the permanent structure of the government — the offices, the powers, and the procedures. Nothing on this site is about the current occupants of those offices, and no part of it argues for one policy or party over another.

## Where the material comes from

The primary source is the Constitution itself, principally Articles I, II, and III, together with the amendments that changed how the branches operate — among them the 12th, 17th, 22nd, and 25th. The framers' own explanations in the Federalist Papers, especially Nos. 47, 51, and 78, are quoted where they clarify why a rule exists.

Structural details that live in statute rather than in the Constitution are noted as such. The size of the House, the number of Supreme Court justices, and the existence of the courts of appeals are all set by Congress and have all changed over time.

## What it leaves out

Three areas are outside the scope of this guide, even though they matter enormously in practice:

- **State and local government.** Every state has its own constitution and its own three branches. This guide covers the federal level only.
- **Political parties.** Parties appear nowhere in the Constitution, but they shape nearly everything about how the branches actually interact.
- **Current events.** Rulings, elections, and legislation change constantly. The structure described here has been stable for far longer.

## Corrections

Civics explanations go stale, and details like chamber procedure are easy to get subtly wrong. If you find an error, open an issue in the repository that builds this site.
