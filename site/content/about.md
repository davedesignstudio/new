## Why three branches?

When the framers wrote the U.S. Constitution in 1787, they had just fought a
revolution against a king who held nearly unlimited power. Their central worry
was simple: **how do you build a government strong enough to function, but not
so strong that it becomes tyrannical?**

Their answer was the **separation of powers**. Instead of concentrating
authority in one place, the Constitution splits the federal government into
three co-equal branches, each described in its own article:

- **Article I — the Legislative branch** makes the laws.
- **Article II — the Executive branch** carries out the laws.
- **Article III — the Judicial branch** interprets the laws.

## Checks and balances

Separating the powers was only half the design. Each branch was also given
tools to *check* the others, so that no single branch can dominate. The
president can veto legislation, Congress can override that veto and impeach
officials, and the courts can strike down laws and actions that violate the
Constitution. This overlapping system is known as **checks and balances**.

The result is a government that moves deliberately, by design. Cooperation and
compromise between the branches are not a bug — they are the entire point.

This is a small, static educational site built with
