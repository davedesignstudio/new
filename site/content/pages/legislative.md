Established by **Article I** of the Constitution, the legislative branch is responsible for **making the laws** of the United States. It consists of the **Congress**, a bicameral (two-chamber) body made up of the Senate and the House of Representatives.

## The two chambers of Congress

### The Senate

Each of the 50 states elects **two Senators**, for a total of 100. Senators serve staggered six-year terms, so roughly one-third of the Senate is up for election every two years. The Senate alone confirms presidential appointments — such as federal judges, ambassadors, and Cabinet secretaries — and ratifies treaties by a two-thirds vote. The Vice President serves as President of the Senate and casts a vote only to break a tie.

### The House of Representatives

The House has **435 voting members**, apportioned among the states by population and redrawn after each census. Representatives serve two-year terms, and every seat is on the ballot in each even-numbered year. The House holds two special powers: all bills that raise revenue must originate there, and it alone may bring **articles of impeachment** against federal officials.

## Key powers

- Draft, debate, and pass federal legislation
- Levy taxes and control federal spending — the **&ldquo;power of the purse&rdquo;**
- Regulate interstate and foreign commerce
- Declare war and raise and support the armed forces
- Confirm appointments and ratify treaties (Senate)
- Impeach federal officials (House) and conduct impeachment trials (Senate)
- Override a presidential veto with a two-thirds vote in both chambers

## How a bill becomes a law

1. A member of Congress introduces a bill.
2. Committees study, amend, and vote on the bill.
3. The full chamber debates and votes; the bill must pass both the House and the Senate in identical form.
4. The President signs the bill into law — or vetoes it.
5. Congress may override a veto with a two-thirds majority in each chamber.

## Checks on the other branches

Congress can **override presidential vetoes**, refuse to confirm appointments or ratify treaties, control funding for executive agencies, and **impeach and remove** the President, federal judges, and other officials. It can also propose constitutional amendments to override Supreme Court interpretations of the Constitution.
