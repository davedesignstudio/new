Nobody can find every library in existence in all of time. That is the first true sentence. Temple chests, palace archives, monastery cupboards, school carts, parish hours, phone backups, a bun stall with a ledger — most of them never sat for a portrait.

IFLA’s Library Map of the World counts libraries in the **millions** once you include public, school, academic, national, and special. Tomorrow some will open. Some will close. A complete list would be a live census of Earth, updated every second, and it would still miss the oral.

This page is a Pinakes of **named** storehouses: enough to show that Alexandria was not the only hunger, and that storage is older than Greek. The deep sitting with one of them is [Alexandria](/alexandria). The attempt to count the books inside is [Holdings](/holdings). Public-domain English of the old mouths: [Mouths](/mouths). The craft conversation is [Libraries](/libraries).

## Clay, palm, papyrus

| Storehouse | Where | When | What the record still says |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Archives of Ebla, Mari, Ugarit | Syria / Mesopotamia | 3rd–2nd millennium BCE | Palace and temple tablets. Administration first; literature in the same clay. |
| Library of Ashurbanipal | Nineveh | 7th c. BCE; burned 612 BCE | Oldest systematically organized library we can still point at. Tens of thousands of tablets recovered; the fire baked some of them into survival. *Gilgamesh* sat here. |
| Qin palace archives | Xianyang | to 206 BCE | Court kept; other books burned in the literary inquisition. Xiang Yu later burned the palace. Storage as control is still storage — and still a fortress. |
| Nalanda | Magadha (India) | Gupta–Pala; sacked c. 1193 | A university of Buddhist (and other) learning. Later tellings say the stack burned for months. Treat the months as legend’s volume knob; the loss is real. |
| Mouseion and Serapeum | Alexandria | from c. 3rd c. BCE | Universal hunger. Pinakes. Not one fire. [The tables](/alexandria). |
| Library of Pergamum | Pergamon | Attalid, 2nd c. BCE | Rival stack. Plutarch’s 200,000 is a boast, not a shelf-check. Story: Antony packed rolls for Cleopatra. |
| Library of Celsus | Ephesus | 110s CE | A tomb that was also a reading room. The façade still stands; the rolls do not. |
| Villa of the Papyri | Herculaneum | buried 79 CE | The only Greco-Roman library that still exists as a room of rolls — carbonized, mostly unread. |
| Imperial libraries of Luoyang and later capitals | China | Han and after | Catalogued in four treasuries: classics, history, masters, collections. Burned and rebuilt with the dynasties. |

## Paper, ink, woodblock

| Storehouse | Where | When | What the record still says |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Dunhuang Cave 17 | Mogao, China | sealed c. 11th c.; opened 1900 | A walled-in stack of tens of thousands of manuscripts. The Diamond Sutra of 868 — earliest dated printed book we can still hold a picture of. |
| Al-Qarawiyyin | Fez | founded 859 by Fatima al-Fihri | Among the oldest working library-mosque-universities. Manuscripts stolen and worn as well as kept. |
| House of Wisdom | Baghdad | Abbasid; wrecked 1258 | Translation house and academy. Ibn al-Nadim’s *Fihrist* is a Pinakes in Arabic. Mongol sack: ink-in-the-Tigris is a trope and a grief. Tusi got some books out to Maragheh. |
| Al-Hakam II’s library | Córdoba | 10th c.; purged 976 | A caliphal stack; “ancient science” later stripped in a surge of orthodoxy. |
| Imperial Library of Constantinople | Constantinople | Byzantine; blows in 1204, 1453, and neglect | Greek and Roman copying house for a millennium. Crusaders and later sieges took what fire did not. |
| Tripitaka Koreana | Haeinsa | 13th c. | 81,352 woodblocks. A library you can print again. Completeness as recarving. |
| Yongle Dadian / Siku Quanshu | Ming / Qing China | 1403; 1772–1780s | Encyclopedic court stacks. Qianlong’s Four Treasuries: seven manuscript copies in seven halls. Some halls burned. Completeness as inquisition too: what was left out was also a catalog. |
| Sankore and the families of Timbuktu | Timbuktu | from c. 14th c.; threatened 2013 | Manuscripts in private houses as much as in institutes. In 2013, people moved hundreds of thousands of volumes out of the fire’s way. A living library is a person with a cart. |
| Vatican Apostolic Library | Rome | medieval core; still open | One of the longest continuous scholarly stacks in Europe. |
| Bodleian, Bibliothèque nationale, legal-deposit houses | Oxford, Paris, and after | early modern → | The idea that a country should keep a copy of what it prints. See [Holdings](/holdings). |
| Maya screenfolds | Yucatán and highlands | until 1562 | Bishop Diego de Landa burned books at Maní. A handful of codices remain. Almost a whole literature reduced to a gutter. |

Monastic rooms — Bobbio, Monte Cassino, St Gall, Lindisfarne — copied Europe through weather. University rooms — Bologna, Paris, Oxford — turned the copy into a school. Carnegie rooms and public systems turned the copy into a loan. None of those sentences is a complete list.

## Rooms that were wrecked on purpose

A short grief, not a complete war-list. Leuven 1914 and 1940. The Institut für Sexualwissenschaft, Berlin, 1933. National Library of Serbia, 1941. Warsaw’s Krasiński and Załuski holdings, 1944. Oriental Library, Shanghai, 1932. National and University Library, Sarajevo, 1992. Iraq National Library, 2003. Mosul, 2015. Ahmed Baba Institute, Timbuktu, 2013 — most of the manuscripts had already been walked out.

Qin burned books to own the past. De Landa burned books to own the soul. Nazis burned books to own the body. The Hidden Parts on this road are the opposite move: **store, don’t bury.**

## The unnamed majority

Every school cart. Every church-basement hour. Every train-station paperback swap. Every kitchen shelf of stained pages. Every phone. Every brain that can still recite.

Athenaeus seated his philosophers at an endless dinner and let them quote lost books from memory. The living library is older than the dead one. Alexandria’s Mouseion fed scholars at a common table so the stacks would have mouths.

**What we took.** Named rooms are not the only rooms. A child at a mill is a stack of one memory. The Reader keeps fragments, not a plaque for every shelf on Earth.

**What we refused.** A fake complete directory. A map that only has palaces on it. Fortune as ethnicity. Completeness as a spear.
