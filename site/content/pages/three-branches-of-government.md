## The Three Branches of the United States Government

The U.S. Constitution divides the federal government into three branches to ensure no single person or group holds too much power. Each branch has its own distinct responsibilities, and a system of **checks and balances** allows each branch to limit the powers of the others.

---

### 1. The Legislative Branch

**Established by Article I of the Constitution.**

The legislative branch makes the laws. It consists of Congress, which is divided into two chambers:

- **The Senate** — 100 members, two from each state, serving six-year terms.
- **The House of Representatives** — 435 members, apportioned by state population, serving two-year terms.

Key powers include drafting and passing legislation, declaring war, regulating commerce, controlling federal spending, and confirming or rejecting presidential appointments and treaties (Senate).

---

### 2. The Executive Branch

**Established by Article II of the Constitution.**

The executive branch enforces and carries out the laws. It is led by:

- **The President** — head of state, commander-in-chief of the armed forces, serving a four-year term.
- **The Vice President** — supports the President and serves as president of the Senate.
- **The Cabinet and federal agencies** — advise the President and administer the day-to-day work of government.

Key powers include signing or vetoing legislation, commanding the military, negotiating treaties, appointing federal judges and officials, and granting pardons.

---

### 3. The Judicial Branch

**Established by Article III of the Constitution.**

The judicial branch interprets the laws and applies them to individual cases. It consists of:

- **The Supreme Court** — the highest court in the nation, with nine justices appointed for life.
- **Lower federal courts** — including courts of appeals and district courts.

Key powers include reviewing laws and executive actions for constitutionality (judicial review), settling disputes between states, and interpreting the meaning of federal law.

---

## Checks and Balances

Each branch can restrain the others. For example:

- The President can **veto** laws passed by Congress.
- Congress can **override a veto** with a two-thirds vote and can **impeach** the President or federal judges.
- The Supreme Court can declare laws or executive actions **unconstitutional**.
- The President **appoints** federal judges, but the Senate must **confirm** them.

This separation of powers protects liberty by preventing any one branch from becoming too powerful.
