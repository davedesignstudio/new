# Three Branches of Government

The Constitution of the United States divides the federal government into three
separate branches. Each one is created by its own article, holds its own powers,
and is limited by the other two — an arrangement known as the separation of
powers.

<div class="branches">
  <article class="branch">
    <h2 class="branch-name">Legislative</h2>
    <p class="branch-source">Article I</p>
    <p class="branch-who"><strong>Who:</strong> Congress — the Senate (100 members) and the House of Representatives (435 members).</p>
    <p class="branch-role">Writes the laws.</p>
    <ul class="branch-powers">
      <li>Drafts and passes bills</li>
      <li>Levies taxes and controls federal spending</li>
      <li>Declares war and funds the armed forces</li>
      <li>Confirms appointments and ratifies treaties (Senate)</li>
      <li>Impeaches and removes federal officials</li>
    </ul>
  </article>

  <article class="branch">
    <h2 class="branch-name">Executive</h2>
    <p class="branch-source">Article II</p>
    <p class="branch-who"><strong>Who:</strong> The President, the Vice President, the Cabinet, and the federal agencies.</p>
    <p class="branch-role">Carries out the laws.</p>
    <ul class="branch-powers">
      <li>Signs bills into law or vetoes them</li>
      <li>Enforces federal law through the agencies</li>
      <li>Commands the armed forces</li>
      <li>Conducts foreign policy and negotiates treaties</li>
      <li>Nominates judges, ambassadors, and Cabinet officers</li>
    </ul>
  </article>

  <article class="branch">
    <h2 class="branch-name">Judicial</h2>
    <p class="branch-source">Article III</p>
    <p class="branch-who"><strong>Who:</strong> The Supreme Court and the lower federal courts.</p>
    <p class="branch-role">Interprets the laws.</p>
    <ul class="branch-powers">
      <li>Decides cases arising under federal law and the Constitution</li>
      <li>Reviews laws and executive acts for constitutionality</li>
      <li>Resolves disputes between states</li>
      <li>Sets binding precedent for the lower courts</li>
      <li>Serves during good behavior, insulating judges from politics</li>
    </ul>
  </article>
</div>

## Checks and balances

No branch acts alone. Each one holds powers that can stop the other two, which
is what keeps any single branch from dominating the government.

<div class="checks">
  <table>
    <thead>
      <tr>
        <th>Branch</th>
        <th>Checks the legislative</th>
        <th>Checks the executive</th>
        <th>Checks the judicial</th>
      </tr>
    </thead>
    <tbody>
      <tr>
        <th scope="row">Legislative</th>
        <td class="none">&mdash;</td>
        <td>Overrides vetoes, controls funding, impeaches</td>
        <td>Confirms or rejects judges, sets court jurisdiction</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <th scope="row">Executive</th>
        <td>Vetoes legislation, calls special sessions</td>
        <td class="none">&mdash;</td>
        <td>Nominates judges, grants pardons</td>
      </tr>
      <tr>
        <th scope="row">Judicial</th>
        <td>Strikes down unconstitutional laws</td>
        <td>Strikes down unconstitutional actions</td>
        <td class="none">&mdash;</td>
      </tr>
    </tbody>
  </table>
</div>

A bill shows the cycle in miniature: Congress passes it, the President signs or
vetoes it, and the courts decide what it means when someone challenges it.
