Nobody can put every text into English. That is the first true sentence. A complete English of Earth would be a spear, a copyright crime, and a lie about time. Living books still have living mouths. Dead books still have translators who have not been dead long enough. This page plates **public-domain** scraps we actually read: old English of old Greek and Latin, short enough to taste, cited so you can walk the rest of the roll yourself.

A **mouth** is the story a person actually tells. A holding is a copy. A title is a hope that the copies are the same work. English is another mouth. Callimachus already knew to list a doubt.

The Mouseion we sat with is [Alexandria](/alexandria). Named rooms through time are [Stacks](/stacks). The count that cannot finish is [Holdings](/holdings). Craft with living tellers is [Libraries](/libraries).

## The keeper’s hunger

The earliest long story of the Alexandrian stack is the *Letter of Aristeas* — a second-century BCE tale, not a shipping log. H. St. J. Thackeray Englished it in 1917. In his own footnote he already corrects the letter: Demetrius of Phalerum **was never the royal librarian**. The English mouth arguing with the Greek mouth. That is collation. Keep it.

> Demetrius of Phalerum, as keeper of the king's library, received large grants of public money with a view to his collecting, if possible, all the books in the world; and by purchases and transcriptions he to the best of his ability carried the king's purpose into execution.

> Being asked once in my presence, about how many thousands of books were already collected, he replied “More than two hundred thousand, O king; and I will ere long make diligent search for the remainder, so that a total of half a million may be reached.”

> Translation is also required.

A later scrap in the same letter pretends to be Demetrius’s memorial:

> In obedience to thy order, O king, that the books which are wanting to complete the library should be added to the collection, and that those which are defective should be duly repaired…

> …in order that we may test wherein the more part agree, and so obtaining an accurate translation may deposit it in a conspicuous place.

Jerome, reading Aristeas later, refused the miracle of seventy identical cells:

> they were assembled in a single hall and conferred together, not that they prophesied.

**What we learned.** Completeness is a king’s grant, not a packing list. Two hundred thousand and half a million are boasts in the same breath. Translation is not a courtesy; the mouths are not already one. The honest method is *wherein the more part agree* — compare, then plate. Fortune that claims every cell wrote the same word is a fortress. Zorya deals. She does not prophesy a complete library of the soul.

Read the rest: Thackeray, *The Letter of Aristeas* (SPCK, 1917), on [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/theletterofarist00unknuoft).

## The table inside the palace

Strabo walked Alexandria around 20 BCE, decades after Caesar’s dock fire. H. C. Hamilton and W. Falconer Englished him in 1854–57. He names the **Museum**. He does not name a separate Library. The stack, if it still sat, sat inside the meal.

> The Museum is a part of the palaces. It has a public walk and a place furnished with seats, and a large hall, in which the men of learning, who belong to the Museum, take their common meal. This community possesses also property in common; and a priest, formerly appointed by the kings, but at present by Cæsar, presides over the Museum.

He also keeps the city’s founding omen. They ran out of chalk tracing the walls. They used supper:

> …the dispensers of flour supplied the workmen with a part of the flour, which was provided for their own use; and this substance was used in tracing the greater part of the divisions of the streets. This, they said, was a good omen for the city.

**What we learned.** The research house was a **kitchen inside a fortress**. Shared dining. Seats. A walk. Property in common. Then empire appointed the priest. That is The Tower in one street: a table that armored itself, then let Caesar name who sits at the head. Flour founded the map. Food is the clue, not the garnish. Strabo still ate there after the docks burned. So it was not one night.

Read the rest: Strabo, *Geography* 17.1.6–8, Hamilton & Falconer, on [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0239%3Abook%3D17%3Achapter%3D1%3Asection%3D8).

## Forty thousand for show

Seneca, *Of Peace of Mind*, Englished by Aubrey Stewart in 1889. He is quoting Livy, and then refusing him:

> Forty thousand books were burned at Alexandria: some would have praised this library as a most noble memorial of royal wealth, like Titus Livius, who says that it was “a splendid result of the taste and attentive care of the kings.” It had nothing to do with taste or care, but was a piece of learned luxury, nay, not even learned, since they amassed it, not for the sake of learning, but to make a show… Let a man, then, obtain as many books as he wants, but none for show.

**What we learned.** A number big enough to sound like supper. Forty thousand, two hundred thousand, seven hundred thousand, 129,864,880, three and a half billion holdings: all true in their own units, none of them a meal. Seneca’s insult is the kitchen’s test. Books as dining-room paint are a fortress of titles. This road keeps few mouths and walks them kindly. Sunday’s number is already late.

Read the rest: [Stewart on Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Of_Peace_of_Mind).

## Fire from the docks

Plutarch, *Caesar* 49, Englished by Bernadotte Perrin in 1919 (the Loeb mouth; old enough to pass). One sentence for the stack. Then a sentence for what Hunger saved:

> …when the enemy tried to cut off his fleet, he was forced to repel the danger by using fire, and this spread from the dockyards and destroyed the great library.

> …he was holding many papers in his hand and would not let them go, though missiles were flying at him and he was immersed in the sea, but held them above water with one hand and swam with the other.

Cassius Dio, another mouth, does not say “the great library.” He says dockyards and storehouses of grain and books by the water. Florus and Lucan barely give the fire houses. Strabo still sits at the common meal.

**What we learned.** It was not one fire. Plutarch’s English is one plate. Hunger kept its own scrap dry while the public stack burned. We already refused the harbor rule: we do not keep your original and send you a copy. We also refuse Caesar’s swim: we do not save the king’s papers and call the kitchen lost.

Read the rest: [Perrin on Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0244%3Achapter%3D49).

## What we will not plate

**Galen**, later, tells the harbor story in Greek: books taken from incoming ships, originals kept, copies sent back, rolls marked *from the ships*; Athens’ tragic three borrowed against fifteen talents, the old mouths kept, the silver forfeited. That anecdote already sits on [Alexandria](/alexandria). The surviving English of that commentary is modern. We will not dump it here. The lesson is older than the copyright: a fortress of books.

**Callimachus’s Pinakes** survive as fragments. Witty’s 1958 English of those fragments is not ours to pour out. What we kept without stealing: a person, a first line, a doubt. Law, rhetoric, miscellaneous. The rest is a later kitchen guessing from crumbs.

**Every book on Earth, in English.** No. Living novels are not a king’s library. WorldCat is not a translation desk. Google’s 129 million was unique printed books, *until Sunday*, not an English of every mouth. If you want a roll that is free to pass, walk [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/), [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/), [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/), [Project Gutenberg](https://www.gutenberg.org/). Then sit. Do not pretend you finished.

## What this road does with those mouths

**What we took.**

- **Translation is also required.** Road-Wisdom turns halves until a picture meets. That is *wherein the more part agree*, not a curse.
- **Confer, do not prophesy.** Jerome against the seventy cells. Zorya against a complete catalog of you.
- **A table inside the palaces.** The Tower was a kitchen that armored itself. The Star is the common meal with the armor off.
- **Flour traces the city.** Food founds the map.
- **Store, don’t bury.** Defective books repaired. Hidden Parts kept. Seneca’s few authors, walked, not stacked for show.
- **List the doubt.** Thackeray’s footnote. Callimachus’s ascription. The Reader’s soldier who was never there.

**What we refused.**

- Every text in English. That is soup. It is also a lie.
- A miracle of identical mouths. Contamination keeps wonder alive. Zipes already said it. Alexandria’s best Homer was many Homers.
- The harbor rule. The king’s dry papers. Completeness as a spear.
- Fortune as ethnicity. The Reader is a fictional traveling mouth. She deals.
