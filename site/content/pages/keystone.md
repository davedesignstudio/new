We did not invent this. It was told to a child who told a child who garnished it. Sometimes the stone is a nail. Sometimes an axe, a button, a chip of wood. The village is always the village that will not share.

## Stone soup

Hungry strangers come with a pot and nothing else. The houses keep their carrots. So the strangers boil a stone and say the soup is almost ready — it only wants a little garnish. One by one the village puts its pieces in. Then the stone comes out, because it was never food. The meal is what people added.

That is the keystone. Not a Chosen One. Not a fortress. **A stone you do not eat**, which exists so other people remember they have carrots.

The tale itself works like the soup. The pot is the same. The garnish is local. That is how a story survives being passed down: not by being locked in a safe, by being added to.

## What the mother left

In the winter stories a dying mother does not leave gold. She leaves a doll and says: keep it, feed it, ask it, show it to no one. The blessing is a mouth you still have to feed. When the girl tells the witch that her mother’s blessing helped her, the witch cannot stand a blessed person in the house and puts her out — with a lantern.

Somebody being there is the job. The doll eats a little. So does the extra chair. So does the name on a bun that would not sell.

## The extra place

Some tables keep a cup no one claims. Some keep a chair that is wrong by one. The count is the fortress. The spare is the keystone. A table that always has one extra seat cannot quietly become a list.

## On this road

Path B1 binds you to the hearth-stone — the pack calls it **Keystone Hearth**. That is the stone in the pot. The Tower tried to lock it in a safe and call it the soup. When the keep falls, the stone can come out. Supper remains. That is The Star.

Gary wanted a spear. The old stories wanted a pot.
