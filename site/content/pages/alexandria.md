Nobody can write from all the text held at Alexandria. That is the first true sentence. The [Pinakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pinakes) are gone. The rolls are gone. Papyrus does not keep like a family table. What we can do is what they did: gather copies, compare the mouths, admit the missing lines, and feed the living from the dead store.

This page is a *sillybos* — the scrap tied to a roll. First line. A count of what is still here. A note that “all the books of the world” was always a king’s hunger, not a packing list.

The talk with other modern tellers is [Libraries](/libraries). Named rooms through time are [Stacks](/stacks). The attempt to count every book is [Holdings](/holdings). What Earth stores instead of burying is in [the Hidden Parts](/hidden).

## The Mouseion was a table, then a stack

The Great Library sat inside the **Mouseion**, a shrine of the Muses in the royal quarter of Alexandria, probably taking physical shape under Ptolemy II after groundwork under Ptolemy I. It was not a public browsing room. It was a research house: shared dining, gardens, lecture rooms, shelves of papyrus. Strabo, visiting around 20 BCE, still saw the community. Scholars were paid, housed, fed, and excused from tax so they could sit with the stacks.

A later, smaller store — the “daughter” library — was put in the **Serapeum**, the temple of Serapis, when the palace shelves ran out of room.

They were not the first people to keep writing. Sumer, Nineveh, Babylon, and Greek collections already knew how. The Ptolemies’ difference was appetite: they wanted a repository of *all* knowledge, mostly in Greek, with other traditions pulled in when a ship or an embassy made it possible.

## How writing arrived

Agents bought at the book fairs of Athens and Rhodes. Older copies were preferred, on the theory that fewer hands had copied them. Homeric poems were hunted in many mouths. Tags named the city a roll had come from — Chios, Sinope, Massalia — so two *Iliads* could sit in the same bin without pretending to be one.

Galen, much later, reports a harbor rule: books found on incoming ships were taken, copied, and the **originals kept**. The copies went back to the owners. Those rolls were marked *from the ships*. Another Galen story: Ptolemy III borrowed Athens’ state copies of Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides against a deposit of fifteen talents, sent back fair copies, and kept the old mouths. He forfeited the silver.

The *Letter of Aristeas* — a second-century BCE story, not a shipping log — says Demetrius of Phalerum advised the king to collect the books of the world, and remembers a Greek Torah made for that table. Modern scholars treat the letter as late and partly wrong. The hunger inside it is still the policy: gather everything, then compare.

**What we took.** Storage as a moral act. Compare versions. Tag where a story came from. Do not pretend two kitchens are one recipe.

**What we refused.** Taking the original and sending back a copy. That is a fortress of books. Guest-friendship feeds first; it does not keep the guest’s pot.

## Callimachus’s tables

Callimachus of Cyrene was never head librarian. He still made the tool the stacks needed. The **Pinakes** — *Tables of those who distinguished themselves in every branch of learning and their writings* — ran about **120 scrolls**. They are lost. Fragments and later talk (Pfeiffer’s frr. 429–456; Witty’s readings of them) let us see the habit.

Each entry was a person, then their work:

- name, father, city, a short life, sometimes a nickname
- then the writings: title or **first line**, how many lines, how many rolls
- and a doubt, when the ascription was not trusted

The known classes include **law**, **rhetoric**, and **miscellaneous**. Later reconstructions split poetry from prose and fill the other bins: epic, lyric, tragedy, comedy, history, medicine, mathematics, natural science. Do not treat that list as a surviving floor plan. It is a later kitchen guessing from crumbs.

Zenodotus, the first recorded librarian, had already alphabetized by the **first letter** and collated Homer. Each roll wore a *sillybos*. Aristophanes of Byzantium later used running line-counts as addresses inside a song, and marked Greek so foreign mouths could hear the meter. The catalog pointed to a room. The room held a person. The person held a roll. The roll held a line.

**Pinakes means tables.** Not a spear. A table of who wrote, and whether we still believe they did.

## What sat in the bins — as far as anyone can still say

Anyone who lists every work held at Alexandria is writing a novel. Ancient counts disagree on purpose: forty thousand, two hundred thousand, four hundred thousand mixed rolls plus ninety thousand unmixed, seven hundred thousand. A roll is not a modern book. Many rolls held more than one work. Many works were duplicates. Tzetzes, centuries later, is not a shelf-check.

What we can name without lying:

**Epic and lyric.** Homer above all. Zenodotus made the first critical edition we can still see in later notes; he likely divided *Iliad* and *Odyssey* into twenty-four books each. Aristophanes of Byzantium and Aristarchus of Samothrace tightened the line-count. Homeric papyri change around 150 BCE: extra lines drop out. The Homer you can buy is downstream of that table.

**Drama.** Alexander of Aetolia on tragedy and satyr-play. Lycophron of Chalcis on comedy. The Athenian three — Aeschylus, Sophocles, Euripides — sat there as prized mouths, if Galen’s story is even half true.

**A poem written at the table.** Apollonius of Rhodes, second librarian, wrote the *Argonautica*. It survived whole. That is rare.

**Earth-writing.** Eratosthenes, third librarian, used the stacks — Alexander’s India, Ptolemaic elephant hunts on the African coast — to make geography a measured thing. His *Geographika* is gone. Strabo ate the leftovers. He also measured the earth’s circumference and came uncannily close.

**Medicine and number.** Hippocratic writings were edited and commented. Herophilus and Erasistratus studied the body in the same city. Euclid taught mathematics in Alexandria; putting his *Elements* on a Mouseion shelf-label is tradition, not a surviving tag. Treat the tradition as a neighbor, not a receipt.

**Other tongues.** The library was Greek-first. Reports and later writers remember Egyptian, Babylonian, and other traditions being translated or collected. The Septuagint story lives in Aristeas, not in a packing list. Do not crown a people with a miracle because a king wanted books.

**Doubt as cataloguing.** Callimachus noted when a work might not be by the name on the *sillybos*. That is the most honest thing on the shelf.

## What still feeds

A library dies in the people before it dies in the fire. In 145 BCE Ptolemy VIII drove foreign scholars out. Aristarchus left for Cyprus. Dionysius Thrax took grammar to Rhodes. Apollodorus went to Pergamum. Menecles joked that Alexandria had become teacher to Greeks and everyone else — because the teachers had been expelled.

What survived did not survive as one vault. It survived as **copying**. Didymus “Bronze-Guts,” later in Alexandria, wrote thousands of commentaries that only work if some of the stacks were still there. Athenaeus’s *Deipnosophists* is an endless dinner where men quote lost books over plates. A banquet kept what a shelf could not. Arabic translators, then Latin ones, then monks, carried Euclid, medicine, Aristotle. Storage is a chain of kitchens.

The Homer in a modern schoolroom, the idea that a poem has a correct line-count, the habit of a catalog that names a person before a title — those are Alexandrian leftovers. So is the hunger to own every mouth.

## It was not one fire

Popular tellings want a single blaze and a villain. The sources will not sit still for that.

- **48 BCE.** Caesar’s fire at the docks. Seneca, quoting Livy, says forty thousand rolls. Plutarch says the great library burned. Cassius Dio points at warehouses of grain and books by the water. Strabo still visits the Mouseion decades later. Damage, then a smaller house.
- **270s CE.** Aurelian’s war wrecks the Broucheion, the royal quarter. If the Mouseion still lived, that is a likely death. Diocletian’s siege later hits the same streets.
- **391 CE.** Bishop Theophilus’s people pull down the Serapeum. Whether a working library still sat in that temple is argued. Orosius is the loudest witness that books died with the building. Other writers go quiet, which is also a kind of witness.
- **642 CE.** A late Arabic story has Caliph Umar fueling bath-fires with the residue of pagan wisdom. It appears centuries after the conquest. John Philoponus, named in the tale, was almost certainly already dead. Serious history sets this one aside.

A fortress of books falls the way a fortress of kitchens falls. First nobody is fed to read. Then the quarter burns. Then a later age tells it as one night, because one night is easier to hold than a century of underfunding.

## What this road does with that habit

[Libraries](/libraries) is research into practice: Le Guin, Morrison, McCloud, a light Propp, CYOA, one Lincoln story, Miyazaki steam, compost, feed first. Alexandria is the older table those tellers were already sitting at, whether they named it or not.

**What we took.**

- **Store, don’t bury.** The Hidden Parts are an endless Earth’s Pinakes: what you refused, kept on a scrap, not thrown in the harbor.
- **Fragments, not a census.** The Reader remembers a fork, a child, a soldier’s wagon that was never there. She does not keep 120 scrolls of your biography. Callimachus listed doubts. So does she.
- **Compare mouths.** Road-Wisdom turns halves until a picture meets. That is collation, not prophecy. The fortune lays the next mile the way an edition lays the next copy.
- **A table of persons.** The Pinakes started with who wrote. This kitchen starts with who sits.

**What we refused.**

- A number big enough to sound like supper. Forty thousand and seven hundred thousand are both ancient boasts. Neither is a meal.
- A museum that will not let the tale be retold. Zipes: wonder stays alive by contamination. Alexandria’s best work was contamination — many Homers become a Homer you can share.
- Fortune as a complete library of the soul. Nobody has that catalog. The Mouseion did not either.
- The harbor rule. We do not keep the original and send you a copy. We pass the plate.
