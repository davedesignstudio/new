## The branch with neither purse nor sword

Alexander Hamilton called the judiciary "the least dangerous" branch in Federalist No. 78, because it controls no army and no money. A court order is words on paper; it works only because the other branches and the public treat it as binding. That fragility is exactly why judges were given lifetime tenure and salaries Congress cannot reduce — independence is the only real power the branch has.

## Judicial review

Nowhere does the Constitution explicitly say that courts may void an act of Congress. The Supreme Court claimed that authority for itself in **Marbury v. Madison** (1803), reasoning that if the Constitution is the supreme law, then a statute that contradicts it cannot be law at all, and saying so is simply part of deciding a case.

Judicial review is what turns the Constitution from a statement of principles into an enforceable limit. It is also the power that most directly checks the other two branches, and the one courts are most careful about using.

## Courts only decide real disputes

Federal judges cannot volunteer opinions or review a law because it looks unwise. Article III limits them to actual "cases and controversies," which means someone with a genuine injury has to bring a lawsuit and the dispute has to still be live. Judges also do not choose which questions reach them — the docket is built entirely out of whatever litigants happen to file.

Once a court does decide, the ruling becomes precedent that lower courts must follow, and the reasoning matters as much as the outcome. Congress can respond to a decision interpreting a statute by amending the statute. Overturning a constitutional ruling is far harder: it takes a constitutional amendment, or the Court changing its own mind.
