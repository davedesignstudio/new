## One person, one office

Article II opens with a single sentence that puts all executive power in one pair of hands: "The executive Power shall be vested in a President of the United States of America." The framers debated a plural executive — a committee at the top — and rejected it. They wanted the branch that acts to be able to act quickly, and they wanted voters to know exactly whom to hold responsible when it acted badly.

That concentration is balanced by the fact that the President cannot do the two things that matter most on his or her own: write the law, or pay for anything.

## Enforcement is a policy choice

"Carrying out the law" sounds mechanical, but Congress writes statutes in general terms and leaves agencies to fill in the specifics. When the executive branch decides how to word a regulation, which violations to prosecute first, and where to direct limited inspectors and investigators, it is making real policy inside the boundaries the law sets.

Executive orders work the same way. They are binding instructions to the executive branch itself, not new legislation — an order cannot create a law, spend money Congress has not appropriated, or override a statute, and courts can strike one down that tries.

## Speaking for the country abroad

Foreign affairs is where presidential power runs widest. The President recognizes foreign governments, directs diplomats, and negotiates on behalf of the United States. Even here the design holds: a treaty requires two-thirds of the Senate, and only Congress can formally declare war or fund a sustained military campaign.
