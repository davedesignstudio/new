## Why the framers put Congress first

Article I is the longest article in the Constitution, and that is not an accident. The framers had just fought a war against a distant monarch, and they wanted the branch closest to the people to hold the most consequential powers: the power to make law, the power to tax, and the power to decide what the government may spend.

That last one is often called **the power of the purse**. No federal dollar can be spent unless Congress has appropriated it, which makes appropriations one of the sharpest tools any branch holds over another.

## A deliberately slow design

Congress is bicameral — split into two chambers — because the states could not agree on how representation should work. Large states wanted seats awarded by population; small states wanted every state treated equally. The Great Compromise of 1787 gave each side one chamber.

The result is a legislature that is intentionally hard to move. A bill must pass both chambers in identical form before it can reach the President's desk, so a proposal has to survive two very different political calculations: one chamber that turns over completely every two years, and one that changes only a third at a time.

## Oversight between elections

Lawmaking is only half the job. Congress also supervises the executive branch it funds. Committees hold hearings, subpoena documents and witnesses, question cabinet secretaries, and investigate how agencies actually carry out the laws on the books. When oversight uncovers a serious problem, Congress can respond by rewriting the law, cutting or conditioning funding, or in the most severe cases, beginning impeachment proceedings.
