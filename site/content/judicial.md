## One Supreme Court, and the courts beneath it

Article III creates **one** Supreme Court. Everything else — the map of district courts, the thirteen courts of appeals, the specialized tribunals — exists because Congress chose to “ordain and establish” it.

Federal judges hold office “during good Behaviour.” In practice that means a life term, removable only by impeachment. The design is independence: a judge who must face voters, or a president, every few years is a judge who may trim a ruling to survive.

There is no constitutional number of Supreme Court justices. The Court has had as few as six and as many as ten. It has been nine since 1869.

## What the judicial power is

Courts do not issue advisory opinions. They decide **cases and controversies** — real disputes between adverse parties. Article III extends that power to cases arising under the Constitution and federal law, to disputes between states, to admiralty, and to certain cases involving ambassadors and foreign parties.

When a case is properly before them, federal judges:

- Interpret statutes and regulations
- Apply the Constitution to the facts
- Resolve conflicts among the lower courts
- Issue orders the executive is bound to obey

They do not write the budget, command troops, or introduce bills. Their force is judgment, plus the expectation that the other branches will comply.

## Judicial review

The Constitution never uses the phrase. The practice arrives in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803), when Chief Justice John Marshall held that a provision of the Judiciary Act of 1789 could not enlarge the Court’s original jurisdiction, and that a statute contrary to the Constitution is void.

That is **judicial review**: the power to refuse to give effect to an act of Congress, a state law, or an executive action that violates the Constitution. It is the judiciary’s great check — and the reason confirmation fights are so fierce.

Courts can be wrong. They have been. The remedy is not a presidential rewrite of the opinion. It is a later case, a constitutional amendment, a statute that stays within the Constitution, or — in the extreme — impeachment.

## The federal ladder

Most federal cases begin in a **district court**, with a trial judge and, if needed, a jury. The losing party may appeal to a **circuit court**, where a panel of judges reviews legal error. The Supreme Court is almost entirely a court of **discretion**. It hears a thin slice of petitions — the conflicts and questions it chooses.

State courts handle the vast majority of American litigation. They are not a fourth federal branch. They are a parallel system, bound by the Supremacy Clause when federal law controls, and by the Supreme Court when a federal question is decided.

## How the courts are checked

Congress creates (and can reorganize) the lower courts, sets some of the Supreme Court’s appellate jurisdiction, and must appropriate the judiciary’s budget. The Senate confirms every Article III judge. The President nominates them. Impeachment remains the final, rarely used discipline.

Judges cannot reach out for cases. They wait. That passivity is itself a check.

## The building

The **Supreme Court of the United States** sits across from the Capitol, in a building that was not finished until 1935. For most of the Republic the Court borrowed rooms in the Capitol — a reminder that the third branch was last in the text, last in the city, and never meant to be first in politics.
