## A court the Constitution barely specified

Article III creates the Supreme Court and then leaves the rest to Congress. There is no constitutional number of justices — nine is a statute, not a commandment. There is no constitutional map of circuits. District courts, courts of appeals, and specialized tribunals exist because Congress built them.

What the Constitution *does* fix: federal judges serve during good behavior (in practice, for life), their pay may not be cut while they sit, and their power extends to cases and controversies — real disputes, not advisory opinions.

## How a case climbs

1. **District court.** Trial: facts, evidence, a first judgment.
2. **Circuit court of appeals.** Usually the last stop. Twelve regional circuits plus the Federal Circuit.
3. **The Supreme Court.** Almost all of its docket is discretionary. Four justices must agree to hear a case (`certiorari`). The Court takes a thin slice of the questions that divide the country or the lower courts.

State courts hear most American litigation. They are not a fourth federal branch. They become a federal story when a federal question, a constitutional claim, or a request for Supreme Court review appears.

## Judicial review

The words “judicial review” are not in the Constitution. The logic is. If the Constitution is supreme law, and judges must decide cases arising under it, then a statute that contradicts the Constitution cannot be applied.

Chief Justice John Marshall wrote that conclusion into American practice in *Marbury v. Madison* (1803): “It is emphatically the province and duty of the judicial department to say what the law is.” Later courts extended the same idea to executive acts and to state laws.

## Independence, and its price

Life tenure is a check on the other branches and a risk to the public. Judges need not please a President who appointed them or a Congress that confirmed them. They also cannot be hurried by an election. Removal is the same steep path used for Presidents: impeachment by the House, conviction by the Senate.

Congress still holds structural levers. It can add seats, change jurisdictions (with limits), create or dissolve lower courts, and refuse to confirm nominees. Those levers are crude, which is the point. A court that could be redesigned every session would not be a court.

## What courts will not do

They do not write budgets, command armies, or introduce bills. They wait for a plaintiff with standing, a live controversy, and a question the law can answer. That passivity is a constitutional feature. The least dangerous branch, Hamilton called it in Federalist No. 78 — because it has neither purse nor sword, only judgment.
