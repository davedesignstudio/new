United States Federal Government

Three Branches,
One Republic

The U.S. Constitution deliberately splits power among three co-equal branches. Each has its own job, and each can check the others — so that no single part of government grows too strong.

Explore the branches How they balance
Article I I

Legislative

Congress — it makes the laws.

Made of
The House of Representatives (435 members) and the Senate (100 members).
Core job
Write and pass bills, control federal spending, and declare war.
Signature power
The “power of the purse” — only Congress can raise revenue and fund the government.
Article II II

Executive

The President — it carries out the laws.

Made of
The President, the Vice President, the Cabinet, and the federal agencies.
Core job
Enforce the laws, conduct foreign policy, and command the armed forces.
Signature power
The veto — the President can reject legislation passed by Congress.
Article III III

Judicial

The Courts — it interprets the laws.

Made of
The Supreme Court and the lower federal courts.
Core job
Decide cases and settle what the law and the Constitution actually mean.
Signature power
Judicial review — courts can strike down laws or actions that violate the Constitution.

Checks & Balances

Separating power is only half the design. Each branch also holds tools to restrain the other two.

  • Congress can override a veto, impeach the President, and reject appointments.
  • The President can veto bills and appoints federal judges.
  • The Courts can declare laws and executive actions unconstitutional.
  • Congress confirms judges and can impeach them too.
  • The President enforces — or must abide by — court rulings.
  • The Courts interpret the statutes Congress writes.

An educational overview · Based on Articles I, II, and III of the U.S. Constitution.
