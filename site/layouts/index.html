United States · Articles I, II, and III

Three branches.
One republic.

The Constitution does not give any office the whole of government. It splits power among a legislature that writes law, an executive that carries it out, and a judiciary that says what the law means — then lets each restrain the others.

The design

A government in three parts

Each branch has its own article, its own officers, and its own incentives. Together they are meant to be energetic enough to govern and divided enough to stay free.

Article I

Legislative

Makes the law

Congress — a House of the people and a Senate of the states — writes statutes, raises money, declares war, and watches the other two branches.

Article I · Congress

Congress writes the rules of the game

On the executive
Overrides a veto by two-thirds of both houses, controls funding, confirms (or rejects) officers and treaties in the Senate, and may impeach.
On the judiciary
Creates lower courts, sets the Supreme Court’s size by statute, confirms justices, and may impeach judges for high crimes and misdemeanors.

Article II · The President

The executive turns statutes into action

On the legislature
May veto bills, recommend measures, convene Congress on extraordinary occasions, and set the agenda by commanding public attention.
On the judiciary
Nominates federal judges, including justices of the Supreme Court, and may pardon federal offenses — except in cases of impeachment.

Article III · The courts

Judges say what the law is

On the legislature
May hold an Act of Congress void if it conflicts with the Constitution — the power of judicial review recognized in Marbury v. Madison.
On the executive
May set aside unlawful executive acts and orders when a proper case reaches the court, and may issue writs that bind officers.

From idea to statute

How a bill becomes law

Most proposals die in committee. The ones that live must survive two chambers — and then the President’s desk.

  1. A representative or senator introduces a bill. Revenue bills must start in the House.

  2. A committee studies, amends, or tables the bill. This is where most bills end.

  3. The full chamber debates and votes. Passage usually needs a simple majority.

  4. The second chamber repeats the process. Differences are reconciled, often in conference.

  5. Sign, and it becomes law. Veto, and Congress may override with two-thirds of each house.

In practice

What happens when…

The diagram is abstract until a real collision arrives. Open a scenario to see which branch moves first — and who can answer.

The President vetoes a popular bill

The executive has checked the legislature. Congress may let the bill die, rewrite it, or override the veto with a two-thirds vote in both the House and the Senate. Courts stay out unless a later lawsuit presents a case.

The Senate rejects a Supreme Court nominee

Advice and consent is a hard check. The seat stays empty until the President names someone the Senate will confirm — or a later Senate does. The Court cannot fill its own vacancy.

The Supreme Court strikes down a statute

Judicial review has checked Congress (and often the President who signed the act). Congress may pass a new statute that avoids the constitutional defect, or — with the states — propose an amendment.

The House impeaches a President

Impeachment is a political trial, not a criminal one. The House accuses; the Senate tries, with the Chief Justice presiding. Conviction and removal require two-thirds of senators present.

Congress refuses to fund a program

The power of the purse is the legislature’s sharpest daily tool. An executive cannot spend money Congress has not appropriated. Courts may referee disputes about what a statute already allows.

A President issues a sweeping executive order

Orders can direct the executive branch, but they cannot repeal a statute or invent a power the Constitution withheld. Congress may legislate over the order; courts may void it if a plaintiff has standing.
