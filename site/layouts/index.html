Power, divided.

The Constitution splits the federal government into three coequal branches so no one person — and no one institution — can rule alone.

Why three?

Separation of powers

The Framers had just broken with a king. They wanted energy in government, but they feared concentrated authority more. So they gave each branch a distinct job, then deliberately tangled those jobs together.

Congress may write a law, but it cannot enforce it. The President may command the armed forces, but cannot fund them alone. The courts may strike down an act of Congress, but they cannot raise an army to make that ruling stick.

James Madison put the wager this way: ambition must be made to counteract ambition.

Try a case

Which branch decides?

Six short questions. No scorekeeping for posterity — just a check that the map is clear.

  1. Who can veto a bill passed by Congress?

    The President may sign or veto legislation. Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds vote in both chambers.

  2. Who has the power to declare war?

    Article I gives Congress the power to declare war. The President is commander in chief once forces are in the field.

  3. Who can strike down a law as unconstitutional?

    Federal courts, culminating in the Supreme Court, exercise judicial review — established in practice by Marbury v. Madison (1803).

  4. Who confirms Supreme Court justices?

    The President nominates; the Senate (the upper chamber of Congress) gives advice and consent.

  5. Who is commander in chief of the armed forces?

    Article II vests command of the military in the President — checked by Congress’s power of the purse and to declare war.

  6. Who resolves disputes about what a federal statute means?

    Courts interpret statutes when cases arise. Congress can rewrite the law if it disagrees with a statutory reading.
