The Three Branches of Government

The U.S. Constitution divides federal power between the Legislative, Executive, and Judicial branches — so that no single person or group can rule alone.

Separation of Powers

Why three branches?

Inspired by the French philosopher Montesquieu, the framers of the Constitution feared concentrated power. In 1787 they split the federal government into three co-equal branches, each defined by its own article of the Constitution and each armed with ways to restrain the other two.

Article I · Legislative Branch

Congress makes the laws

The legislative branch is a bicameral Congress that meets at the U.S. Capitol. It writes the nation’s laws, controls its money, and speaks for the people who elect it.

100Senators — 2 per state, serving 6-year terms
435Representatives — seats based on state population, 2-year terms
25 & 30Minimum ages to serve in the House and Senate

Key powers

  • Write, debate, and pass federal laws
  • Levy taxes and control government spending (“power of the purse”)
  • Declare war and fund the armed forces
  • Regulate interstate and foreign commerce
  • Senate confirms judges, ambassadors, and Cabinet officers
  • Impeach (House) and try (Senate) federal officials

How a bill becomes law

  1. A bill is introduced in the House or Senate
  2. Committees study, amend, and vote on it
  3. Both chambers must pass the identical text
  4. The President signs it — or vetoes it
  5. Congress can override a veto with a two-thirds vote in each chamber
Article II · Executive Branch

The President enforces the laws

Headquartered at the White House, the executive branch carries out and enforces federal law through the President, the Vice President, the Cabinet, and about 15 executive departments plus many agencies.

4Years in a presidential term — two-term limit under the 22nd Amendment
35Minimum age; must also be a natural-born citizen
~15Executive departments, from State and Defense to Education

Key powers

  • Enforce and administer federal laws
  • Serve as Commander in Chief of the armed forces
  • Sign bills into law — or veto them
  • Negotiate treaties (with Senate ratification)
  • Appoint federal judges, ambassadors, and Cabinet members
  • Grant pardons for federal offenses

Did you know?

The President is chosen by the Electoral College, not directly by the national popular vote. Each state gets electors equal to its members of Congress; a candidate needs 270 of 538 electoral votes to win.

If the President cannot serve, the line of succession begins with the Vice President, then the Speaker of the House.

Article III · Judicial Branch

The courts interpret the laws

The judicial branch decides what the law and the Constitution mean. At its head sits the Supreme Court, above the federal courts of appeals and district courts.

9Supreme Court justices — one Chief Justice and eight Associate Justices
LifeJustices serve during “good behavior” — effectively lifetime appointments
1803Marbury v. Madison established judicial review

Key powers

  • Interpret the meaning of laws and the Constitution
  • Strike down laws or executive actions that are unconstitutional (judicial review)
  • Resolve disputes between states and cases involving federal law
  • Set nationwide precedent through Supreme Court rulings

Independence by design

Federal judges are appointed, not elected — nominated by the President and confirmed by the Senate. Lifetime tenure and protected salaries let them rule on the law without fear of political retaliation.
